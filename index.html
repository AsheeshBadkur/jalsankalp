Rainwater Harvesting Systems 💧

Welcome to the world of water conservation! Here, we'll explain the different ways you can collect and use rainwater, so you can choose the best system for your home and local environment.

alt="Direct Storage in Tanks">

Direct Storage in Tanks

This is the simplest and most common method, perfect for homeowners who want to immediately reduce their municipal water usage.

What it is:

Rainwater from your rooftop is channeled into a large storage tank. This water is kept clean and safe for non-potable uses. It's a great way to have a ready supply of water for tasks like gardening, flushing toilets, and washing your car.

How to implement it:
  1. Assess Your Needs: Calculate how much water your household uses for non-potable purposes each day.
  2. Install Gutters & Downpipes: Install or use your existing gutters to collect water from the roof.
  3. Set Up a Filter: A simple, low-cost filter is installed before the tank.
  4. Connect the Tank: Place a durable plastic or concrete tank on a solid, level base.
  5. Use the Water: Install a pump or a simple tap to easily access the stored water.
alt="Recharge Pit">

Recharge Pit

This technique focuses on replenishing the groundwater naturally, which is vital for the long-term health of our water table.

What it is:

A recharge pit is a small, deep hole dug into the ground and filled with layers of filter materials like boulders, gravel, and sand. It's designed to collect rainwater from your roof and allow it to slowly seep into the ground, recharging the underground aquifer.

How to implement it:
  1. Find the Right Spot: Choose a location in your yard or open space.
  2. Dig the Pit: Excavate a pit of the recommended size.
  3. Create a Filter Bed: Fill the pit with a graded filter media.
  4. Connect the Pipe: Run a pipe from your rooftop's downpipe directly into the recharge pit.
  5. Cover It Up: Place a perforated concrete slab on top of the pit.
alt="Recharge Trench">

Recharge Trench

A recharge trench is a great alternative to a pit, especially if you have a larger roof or limited space in your yard.

What it is:

A recharge trench is a long, shallow ditch that is filled with the same filter media used in a recharge pit. This design allows for a larger surface area for water to infiltrate the ground, making it very efficient for recharging shallow aquifers.

How to implement it:
  1. Dig the Trench: Based on your calculated dimensions, dig a long, narrow trench.
  2. Build the Filter Bed: Fill the trench with layers of boulders, gravel, and sand.
  3. Connect the System: Route the downpipe from your roof to the trench.
  4. Cover It Safely: Cover the trench with soil or a permeable surface.
alt="Recharge Well">

Recharge Well

For areas where the groundwater level is very deep, a recharge well is the most effective solution.

What it is:

A recharge well is a deep, narrow well that bypasses any non-porous soil layers to send clean rainwater directly into the deep aquifer. This method is often used in areas with hard rock geology where surface-level pits or trenches are not effective.

How to implement it:
  1. Drill the Borewell: A professional must drill a borewell to the recommended depth.
  2. Set Up the Filtration: Filtered water from your rooftop is first collected in a small chamber.
  3. Pipe it Down: A pipe is then run from the surface chamber into the recharge well.
  4. Seal and Monitor: The top of the well is sealed with a perforated cover.

Types of Roofs & Runoff Coefficient ☔

The Runoff Coefficient (C) is a key number in rainwater harvesting. It represents the percentage of rainfall that actually runs off a surface and can be collected. For example, a roof with a runoff coefficient of 0.85 means it can collect 85% of the rain that falls on it. This number varies based on the roof's material, which is why identifying your roof type is the first step.

Corrugated Metal/Plastic Sheets

Runoff Coefficient: 0.90

These roofs are highly efficient for rainwater harvesting. Their smooth surface and sloping design allow water to quickly run off without being absorbed.

What this means: You can expect to collect up to 90% of the rainwater that falls on this type of roof.

Concrete Roof (Default)

Runoff Coefficient: 0.85

This is the most common type of roof on modern homes. A clean, flat concrete surface is very effective at collecting water, though it's slightly less efficient than metal due to a bit of absorption.

What this means: You can expect to collect up to 85% of the rainwater. This is the standard value we use in our calculations.

Clay Tiles

Runoff Coefficient: 0.75

Clay-tiled roofs, often found in traditional homes, also work well for harvesting. The tiles' surface texture can cause slightly more water loss compared to smooth concrete or metal.

What this means: You can expect to collect up to 75% of the rainwater.

Asbestos Sheets

Runoff Coefficient: 0.80

It is extremely important to identify and avoid this type of roof for any rainwater harvesting. Asbestos is a hazardous material, and water collected from it can be contaminated, making it unsafe for any use.

What this means: Even though it has a good runoff coefficient, the water is not safe to collect or use. Your health and safety are the top priority.

Government Policies on Rainwater Harvesting ⚖️

Rainwater harvesting (RWH) is a national priority, and many states and cities across India have made it mandatory for new and existing buildings. Understanding these rules can help you stay compliant and even take advantage of government incentives.

Kerala

Rule: Rainwater harvesting made mandatory in new buildings via Kerala Municipality Building Rules (1999), amended January 12, 2004.

Implementation: Subsidized ~6,000 units (90% subsidy via Kerala Water Authority; 75% via Jalanidhi). Plans to install 8,750 wells for RWH.

New Delhi (NCT)

Rule: Since June 2001, mandatory RWH for buildings with roof area >100 m² or plots >1,000 m². CGWA made it compulsory in notified areas and banned tubewell drilling.

Implementation: Building permissions not granted without RWH, with inspections by DDA/MCD. Delhi Jal Board offers incentives: up to 50% of cost or ₹200,000, plus awards for RWAs.

Indore (Madhya Pradesh)

Rule: Mandatory RWH for buildings ≥250 m². Incentive: 6% rebate on property tax.

Implementation: Dedicated RWH department set up in 2000. Over 3,000 residential and 150 public projects executed. Tax waivers and rebates helped drive high compliance.

Kanpur (Uttar Pradesh)

Rule: RWH compulsory for new buildings with area ≥1,000 m².

Hyderabad (Andhra Pradesh)

Rule: Mandatory RWH for new buildings ≥300 m², including group housing & commercial complexes. Existing buildings required to install within a year of notification.

Implementation: HMWS&SB constructed ~14,000 RWH structures. However, citizen uptake limited—only 500 of 5,000 applications implemented; subsidy program later dropped due to low response.

Tamil Nadu

Rule: Through the 2003 ordinance, RWH made mandatory statewide for all buildings. Building plans and water/sewer connections require RWH in place; applies to all buildings three stories or higher.

Implementation: By late 2003, over 4.8 million private and ~172,000 government buildings had RWH systems. TWAD and CMWSSB provided technical support, awareness campaigns, and enforcement, with disconnection penalties for noncompliance.

Rajasthan

Rule: Mandatory RWH for public establishments and urban properties on plots exceeding 500 m².

Implementation: 6-month compliance window; completion certificates withheld by PHED until RWH is implemented. Water supply can be disconnected if not complied.

Mumbai (Maharashtra)

Rule: Initially mandatory for buildings on plots >1,000 m² (since 2002). Later raised to >3,000 m² and then >5,000 m² by 2007.

Implementation: Water connections are reduced (90 lpcd vs 135 lpcd), encouraging RWH. RWH structures required for occupancy certificates; pilot projects in civic buildings; public awareness campaigns; penalties include withholding certificate or water supply.

Gujarat

Rule: Mandatory RWH in all government buildings. Under development control regulations:

Buildings 500–1,500 m² require RWH;

Buildings 1,500–4,000 m² must provide percolation wells—1 per 4,000 m².

Implementation: Small-scale rollouts in government buildings; Surat Municipal Corporation active in awareness campaigns.

Haryana

Rule: RWH mandatory in all new buildings (regardless of size); CGWA mandates RWH in notified areas and bans tubewell drilling.

Chandigarh

Rule: RWH compulsory for all new buildings, regardless of plot or roof size.

Himachal Pradesh

Rule: Mandatory RWH in all urban buildings, schools, government buildings, industries & bus stands. Building plans not approved without RWH.

Daman & Diu

Rule: Authorities instructed PWD and local bodies to construct rooftop RWH; action has been initiated.

Goa

Rule: PWD directed to implement RWH in government buildings; planning studies underway for existing and new infrastructure.

Benefits of Rainwater Harvesting

Implementing RWH systems provides numerous environmental and economic advantages:

  • Reduces water bills by up to 50%
  • Improves groundwater levels and quality
  • Reduces soil erosion and urban flooding
  • Provides water security during droughts
  • Environmentally friendly and sustainable
  • Reduces demand on municipal water supply
  • Can be used for irrigation, cleaning, and with treatment, for drinking
